Groundwater capstone · Model brief

Rain In, Depth Out

Two regression models, both answering the same question: given how much rain fell, how deep should the water be in this well right now? One is a straight line, one is a tree ensemble. The gap between them, and the gap between both and the rain itself, is the finding.

What is being predicted

Every well is measured four times a year. The target is not the raw depth — a deep well in Rajasthan and a shallow one in Kerala are not comparable — but each reading's distance from that well's own normal for that season, in metres. Positive means deeper than usual, which is worse.

target = depth this November − this well's own average November
209,388 readings · 2,759 wells · 19 states · 2000–2022 · CGWB wells and the IMD 0.25° rainfall grid, nothing else.

Inputs are rainfall windows before the reading date (3, 6, 12, 24 and 36 months of total rain, each also as a percentage of that well's own normal, plus last monsoon, rainy-day count and wettest day), then which season the reading is, the well type and the aquifer. Year, district, latitude, longitude and the well's own normal are blocked by name so the model cannot memorise a place instead of learning from rain.

The two models

Model 1 · the honest baseline
Ridge regression
  • One linear fit per season (January, May, August, November) — the monsoon works differently in each.
  • Penalised coefficients, because the rain windows overlap heavily and would otherwise fight.
  • Readable: every coefficient is "one more mm of rain, this many cm of water".
test skill +0.086
Model 2 · the one deployed
Gradient-boosted trees
  • LightGBM, which can bend: a dry aquifer responds to 500 mm very differently from a wet one.
  • Constrained so more rain can never predict deeper water. Without that guard the scenario map would show absurdities.
  • Powers the "what if the monsoon were bigger" numbers in the atlas.
test skill +0.167

How they were scored

Years are split once and not revisited: 2001–2014 to train, 2015–2017 to choose settings, and 2018–2022 kept sealed until the end. Every well's "normal" is rebuilt inside each fold from training years only, so no future reading leaks backwards.

Skill is the honest yardstick: how much better than simply saying "this well will read its usual value". Zero means the model added nothing. Negative means it made things worse.

Proof it is really using the rain

A model can score well by accident. So the same model was refit three more times on deliberately broken rainfall. If it still scored, the skill was never about rain.

What the model was fedSkill
The real rain+0.077
Rain shuffled between years+0.004
Rain shuffled between wells−0.007
Rain from four years later−0.017

All three collapse to nothing. The four-year shift matters most: the model looks back up to 36 months, so a one-year shift would still overlap real rain and quietly pass.

Why this target, and not another

Three targets were built and scored the same way before one was chosen.

All three carry real rainfall signal, and interestingly the linear model wins on year-to-year change while the trees win on level. The level target was deployed because it has five times the data and the clearest skill. Tuning was not the lever: 24 hyperparameter settings were searched and the whole spread was 0.007 skill — the features decide this model, not the settings.

What these models cannot do

Limit
They cannot forecast rain

Monsoon totals barely relate from one year to the next, so no model here predicts next year's rainfall. The user picks a monsoon size; the model answers for that size.

Limit
They cannot separate pumping

Subtracting the rain-explained part to leave "extraction" was tried and withdrawn: split the record in half and the two halves disagree (−0.31), while plain measured decline agrees (+0.27).

Limit
Skill of 0.17 is modest

Most of what moves a well is local — pumping next door, canal water, the aquifer itself. Rain is one real input among several, and the number says so honestly.
